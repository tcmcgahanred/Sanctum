## NEWS

**20260827-A — Five federal agencies warn of an active campaign using AI-generated scripts to attack Siemens S7 industrial controllers, with water utilities a primary target.** On August 19 the NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy, and Environmental Protection Agency issued a joint advisory (AA26-231A) warning of an active threat to internet-exposed Siemens S7 Series programmable logic controllers — the small industrial computers that run pumps, valves, and treatment processes. The advisory covers the full S7 line (S7-200 through S7-1500) and describes threat actors conducting reconnaissance and capability development using AI-generated exploitation scripts disguised as legitimate monitoring tools, reaching devices over the industrial S7comm protocol. The actors use internet-scanning services (Censys, ZoomEye) to find exposed, outdated, or poorly protected controllers. The agencies did not attribute the activity and describe it as persistent reconnaissance to prepare for possible future effects — not, in this advisory, confirmed destructive attacks via this specific method. The EPA's inclusion signals water and wastewater utilities are a primary audience; other named sectors are critical manufacturing, energy, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. *Why an SLTT organization should care: water and wastewater is a core SLTT sector, and this is the exact technology that runs small treatment plants. The priority action is foundational operational-technology hygiene and a vendor/operator-accountability ask: confirm no Siemens S7 controllers are reachable from the public internet, segment control networks from information-technology networks and the internet, and apply CISA's mitigations and indicators. Agencies that outsource water operations should ask their operator to confirm the same.*

It is a concrete instance of exactly the risk the Siemens advisory above describes, and the defensive posture is the same: get operational-technology systems off the public internet and segment them.*

**20260827-C — A cyberattack forced a major public university offline days before classes, delaying the start of its semester.** The University of Texas at San Antonio (about 40,000 students across six campuses) detected threat activity over the weekend of August 16–17 and took systems offline, including course registration, tuition payment, and campus phones; the university delayed the start of fall classes from August 19 to August 24. The university's technology officer said the activity was detected at the edge of the network and contained before reaching core systems, and that the investigation has found no evidence university data was accessed or exfiltrated; a Texas state cyber authority separately characterized the incident as involving a breach. All students, faculty, and staff were required to reset their passwords. *Why an SLTT organization should care: higher education is an SLTT sector, and California's public universities and community colleges face the same exposure — this incident's timing at the start of a semester is exactly when operational pressure is highest. The defensible takeaways are mandatory password resets after a suspected intrusion and the value of edge containment; for peer institutions, it is a prompt to confirm incident-response and continuity plans ahead of their own term starts.*

**20260827-D — Justice Department charges 17 Iranian hackers over a long-running campaign that breached 144 U.S. universities.** The Department of Justice unsealed a 14-count superseding indictment on August 18 charging 17 members of the Iran-based Mabna Institute (tracked by researchers as Silent Librarian / Cobalt Dickens) with a cyber-espionage campaign, conducted in part for Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, that targeted 144 U.S. universities, 178 foreign universities, and five U.S. federal and state government agencies, stealing more than 31 terabytes of academic data and intellectual property. The activity is old but the playbook endures and is defensible: the entire campaign ran on stolen passwords, so multifactor authentication on faculty and staff accounts and phishing awareness (especially fake library and login pages) are the direct countermeasures.*

## CTA TTPs

**20260827-F — Attackers are building application-specific web shells that steal directory credentials, turning one server compromise into network-wide access.** Researchers at ReliaQuest analyzed a custom web shell — linked to the Clop extortion group — that was purpose-built for a specific enterprise application rather than reused from generic tools. After the attackers exploit an internet-facing server, the web shell reads the application's own stored secrets and decrypts them, returning the directory-service (LDAP) administrator password in plain text, and maps the file stores for theft. Because a directory credential typically governs access across email, remote-access VPN, and the wider Windows environment, harvesting it from one compromised application can escalate a single break-in toward the whole organization. (In this case the targeted product was a manufacturing design-management platform not typical of SLTT environments — the point here is the technique, not that specific product.) *Why an SLTT organization should care: the pattern is what matters — once attackers are on one internet-facing application, they increasingly go straight for stored directory credentials to move everywhere, so patching that one app is not the end of the response. The defensible measures are general: do not store high-privilege directory credentials where an application can decrypt them in plain text, rotate credentials after any server compromise, and segment networks so a single application server cannot reach the entire directory. This is the same credential-theft-to-escalation pattern seen in the SonicWall/Akira intrusion reported earlier this month.*
**Score:** [2.25] · out-of-portfolio product, included as generalizable adversary tradecraft
*Sources: [BleepingComputer, "Clop created custom web shell for Windchill data theft attacks," Aug 2026](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/clop-created-custom-web-shell-for-windchill-data-theft-attacks/); [ReliaQuest, "Clop Returns with Custom Implant in Mass-Extortion Campaign," Aug 2026](https://reliaquest.com/blog/clop-returns-with-custom-implant-in-mass-extortion-campaign/).*

## LATEST ATTACKS OR RISKS

**20260827-G — CISA confirms ransomware gangs are now exploiting a Windows Task Host privilege-escalation flaw.** CISA updated its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog to confirm ransomware groups are abusing a high-severity Windows Task Host vulnerability (CVE-2025-60710). The flaw — a "link-following" weakness in a core Windows background-task component affecting Windows 11 and Windows Server 2025 — lets a local attacker with basic user permissions escalate to SYSTEM (full control of the device). Microsoft patched it in November 2025; CISA flagged it as actively exploited in April 2026 and has now confirmed ransomware use. *Why an SLTT organization should care: this affects near-universal Windows software and gives attackers full control after they gain an initial foothold. The patch has been available since November 2025, so this is a patch-prioritization message: as one analyst observed, organizations hurt by flaws like this usually had the patch available and simply had not gotten to it, because they prioritized by severity score rather than by what is actively being used in attacks. Confirm the November 2025 update is applied, and ask any information-technology provider to confirm the same.*
**Score:** [4.5] · Tier 3 exploited flaw in SLTT-common technology · ×1.5 exploited/active · ×1.3 ransomware vs public-sector/critical infrastructure · force-surfaced (M2)
*Source: [BleepingComputer, "CISA: Windows Task Host flaw now exploited by ransomware gangs," Aug 2026](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/cisa-windows-task-host-flaw-now-exploited-by-ransomware-gangs/).*
